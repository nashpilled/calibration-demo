stats

yes/no questions — were your probabilities honest?
Each dot groups your answers by the probability you stated (dot size = how many). A dot on the dashed line means events you called "70%" happened 70% of the time. Below the line = overconfident, above = underconfident.
range questions — how often your 90% ranges catch the truth
Dot = observed catch rate (hits/asked shown per row); bar = 95% confidence given that many questions. Well-calibrated play sits on the red 0.90 line — left of it your ranges are too narrow. Wide bars just mean few questions so far.
how to read these numbers
nI / nT
How many range (interval) and yes/no (threshold) questions you have answered. Everything else is computed from these — treat rows with tiny counts as noise.
hit% and ±SE
The share of your 90% ranges that contained the truth. Perfect calibration is 90: below it your ranges are too narrow (overconfident), above it too wide. ±SE is the statistical wobble from the sample size — a hit% within about two SE of 90 is indistinguishable from calibrated.
score mean / med
The interval score, in factors of 10: your range's width in decades, plus a penalty of 20 per decade the truth landed outside it. Lower is better; it rewards ranges that are narrow and catch the truth. Mean ≫ median means a few blowout misses are dragging you — the median is what your typical answer looks like.
width|hit
Average width (in decades) of the ranges that did catch the truth. 1.0 means your typical successful range spanned a full factor of 10. The game: shrink this without letting hit% fall below 90.
med.bias
Among your catches, the fraction where the truth came in above your stated median. 0.50 is unbiased; above 0.5 you habitually guess low, below 0.5 you guess high. (This is the bias the question generator exploits when placing yes/no cutoffs.)
brier
Mean squared error of your stated probabilities: (p − outcome)². 0 is perfect, 0.25 is what always answering "50%" scores, 1 is confidently wrong every time. Lower is better.
murphy cal
The slice of the Brier score caused by miscalibration — saying "80%" for things that happen 60% of the time. 0 means every probability you use is honest. Lower is better.
murphy res
Resolution: how strongly your probabilities separate the things that happened from the things that didn't. Higher is better; 0 means your answers carry no information beyond the base rate. You improve by pushing cal down and res up — always saying "50%" gets cal ≈ 0 but res = 0, which is safe and useless.
murphy unc
Uncertainty: the base rate's own variance, ō(1 − ō), where ō is the share of yes-outcomes. It measures how hard the question set is, not your skill — you can't move it by answering better, only the question mix can. Its cap is 0.25 (a 50/50 set). It completes the decomposition, which the three columns satisfy exactly: brier = cal − res + unc — so res can never exceed unc, and unc is the score you'd get by always answering the base rate.
grade
One composite mark for your range play: 55% calibration (hit% near 90), 25% sharpness (narrow successful ranges), 10% bias (medians centered), 10% consistency (no blowout misses dragging the mean). Hover the chip for the four factors. ·prov means fewer than 10 answers — read it as noise until then. Calibration dominates by design: an A requires honesty first.
betting P&L
After each stated probability, the market quotes a line with a small spread and you take YES, take NO, or pass. Correct play: bet only when your belief clears the spread — passing near your own belief is right, not timid. The line is set from your historical calibration (never this question's answer), tilted so that if your "70%"s really come true 60% of the time, you are offered bets your stated beliefs love and your true hit rate loses. A calibrated player breaks even or better; negative P&L is the cash value of your miscalibration, at stake 1 per bet.
