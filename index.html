yes/no questions — how did probabilities compare with outcomes?
Each dot groups your answers by the probability you stated (dot size = how many). A dot on the dashed line means events you called "70%" happened 70% of the time. Below the line means YES was overestimated; above means YES was underestimated. Bars show descriptive 95% Wilson intervals; small or adaptive samples need caution.
range questions — how often your 90% ranges catch the truth
Dot = observed catch rate (hits/asked shown per row); bar = 95% confidence given that many questions. Well-calibrated play sits on the red 0.90 line — left of it your ranges are too narrow. Wide bars just mean few questions so far.
how to read these numbers
nI / nT
How many range (interval) and yes/no (threshold) questions you have answered. Everything else is computed from these — treat rows with tiny counts as noise.
hit% and ±SE
Observed coverage; the target is 90%. Small samples fluctuate. Use the displayed Wilson interval rather than treating a point estimate or ±SE as proof of calibration. Repeated items and adaptive selection limit its interpretation.
median error
Absolute log error |log10(m) − log10(truth)|. This rewards reporting your predictive median. Lower is better; a factor-of-ten error costs 1.
score mean / med
The interval score, in factors of 10: your range's width in decades, plus a penalty of 20 per decade the truth landed outside it. Lower is better; it rewards ranges that are narrow and catch the truth. Mean ≫ median means a few blowout misses are dragging you — the median is what your typical answer looks like.
width|hit
Average log width conditional on a hit. This can reward selection effects, so it is a legacy diagnostic. Compare proper mean interval scores; the new mean width uses every interval.
med.bias
Legacy fraction of hits with truth above the reported median. It is conditional on hits, so it is not an unconditional median-calibration test. The new below/equal/above diagnostics use every interval. The local generator uses the evidence-qualified median direction.
brier
Mean squared error of your stated probabilities: (p − outcome)². 0 is perfect, 0.25 is what always answering "50%" scores, 1 is confidently wrong every time. Lower is better.
murphy cal
Empirical reliability error grouped by exact stated probability. Small or unique probability groups can inflate it even for a calibrated forecaster. A nonzero value does not imply dishonesty. The letter band is hidden while sample support is sparse.
murphy res
Empirical separation of realized rates among exact-probability groups. Singleton groups can spuriously suggest full resolution. This is descriptive, not an out-of-sample estimate of skill. Constant 50% forecasts are calibrated only on a 50% event base rate.
murphy unc
Uncertainty: the base rate's own variance, ō(1 − ō), where ō is the share of yes-outcomes. It measures how hard the question set is, not your skill — you can't move it by answering better, only the question mix can. Its cap is 0.25 (a 50/50 set). It completes the decomposition, which the three columns satisfy exactly: brier = cal − res + unc — so res can never exceed unc, and unc is the score you'd get by always answering the base rate.
grade
Legacy composite grade, retained for continuity. Its hit-rate and conditional-width rewards are not a proper scoring rule. The primary objectives are mean interval score, absolute log median error, and Brier. Report your real beliefs instead of optimizing the letter.
betting P&L
A separate decision exercise at stake 1. Bet only when your belief exceeds the offered price; otherwise pass. Offers use past calibration in the question domain and never the current truth. Extreme forecasts without history produce no artificial edge. P&L also reflects price spreads, sampling variation, and estimation error; it is not a direct measure of miscalibration.
